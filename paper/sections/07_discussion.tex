\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{What can be concluded about EEG foundation models?}

The study is designed around a narrow but important question: full fine-tuning
performance alone may not identify the source of a foundation model's gains.
The corrected phase-interleaved benchmark shows that a compact generic encoder
can be competitive on several tasks, while the primary-metric deficits on TUAB,
SEED-V, SHU-MI, and Mumtaz2016 reject a universal replacement claim. The
retained EEGNet, BIOT, and LaBraM rows also show why the comparison should be
read as a ladder of architecture and pretraining controls, not as a single
winner-take-all leaderboard. More generally, new
foundation models should compare against strong architecture-and-geometry
controls, not only task-specific networks with weaker transfer recipes.
This interpretation is consistent with recent calls to reassess EEG
foundation models against carefully tuned conventional decoders
\citep{yang2026eegworth}.  The shared conclusion is methodological: stronger
controls are needed before an improvement can be attributed specifically to
large-scale EEG pretraining; it is not evidence that foundation models are
categorically unnecessary.

The matched initialization ablation now isolates one part of this claim. On
both TUEV and PhysioNet-MI, and for all three tested backbones, replacing the
ImageNet/DINOv3 visual weights with random initialization lowers test $\kappa$
by 7.48--27.82 points under the same five-seed, validation-selected protocol.
Balanced accuracy and weighted F1 move in the same direction. Thus the result
is not only that a particular architecture can fit EEG after folding: the
pretrained visual representation itself supplies transferable structure for
EEG fine-tuning. The control still leaves open whether EEG pretraining is
better for frozen, few-shot, unseen-montage, or topology-sensitive transfer.

The expanded comparison also sharpens the role of scale, spatial resolution, and
spatial identity. Several 64-channel tasks remain competitive with native
$P=1$ inputs, whereas low-channel recordings require folding both to preserve signal-bearing
height and to expose phase-structured temporal neighborhoods. This pattern is compatible with a benefit from denser
electrode coverage, but the benchmark is not a controlled electrode-count
study: channel number is confounded with montage, dataset, and task. Increasing
electrode count should therefore be presented as a testable direction rather
than a guaranteed performance improvement.
Against 69M-parameter \reve{}-Base, \method{} is competitive on selected
shared tasks, but the TUEV-version and ISRUC-preprocessing caveats remain
important. The clean PhysioNet-MI and BCIC2020-3 gains show
that large-scale EEG pretraining is not always necessary under full
fine-tuning. TUAB favors the EEG references, while FACED favors ConvNeXt-Tiny
over both EEG references and the other visual backbones. These outcomes
show task and architecture dependence without isolating the contribution of
physical coordinates or EEG pretraining.

In the terminology of our title, vision pretraining is a \emph{conditional}
free lunch: its weights are available without additional EEG pretraining, but
their value depends on input geometry, downstream optimization, and task
characteristics. The mixed pattern across the 12 benchmarks makes this
conditionality empirically visible.

The results do \emph{not} show that EEG pretraining is unnecessary.  This work
does not yet evaluate few-shot learning, missing-channel robustness, frozen
features, cross-montage transfer, or pretraining-data efficiency.  These are
precisely the settings in which large, coordinate-aware EEG models should be
most valuable.  A fair interpretation is that generic visual priors are an
unexpectedly strong component under end-to-end supervised adaptation.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline comparability.}  EEGNet, SPaRCNet, BIOT, LaBraM, \cbramod{},
    and \reve{} are published references, not local paired reproductions. Our
    split definitions reproduce the subject- or trial-level partitions reported
    by \cbramod{} and, where available, \reve{}
    \citep{wang2024cbramod,elouahidi2025reve}; preprocessing, optimization,
    seeds, and model selection remain method-specific. TUEV is a cross-version
    comparison (local v2.0.1 versus published v2.0.0), and REVE--ISRUC uses
    corrected channel selection. For TUAB and TUEV we use \cbramod{}'s
    target-corpus-excluded controls rather than its full-pretraining results.
    \item \textbf{Dataset-specific recipes.}  The encoder is fixed, but $P$,
    optimization, input scale, and augmentation vary by dataset.  This is a
    tuned downstream protocol, not zero-shot universality.  We introduce the
    prespecified geometry rule in Eq.~\ref{eq:p-geometry-rule} to reduce this
    freedom. The formal recipes are evaluated with this fixed geometry rule;
    their benchmark scores do not establish that the selected factors are optimal.
    \item \textbf{Remaining controls.} Matched random initialization is now
    reported on TUEV and PhysioNet-MI, but frozen transfer, identical-pipeline
    EEG-foundation baselines, FLOPs, throughput, and memory are not yet in the
    main table.
    \item \textbf{Statistical uncertainty.} Three to five seeds are informative
    but limited, especially for MentalArithmetic. Subject-level bootstrap
    intervals and paired tests would better reflect evaluation uncertainty.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Broader impact}

A small vision backbone may reduce the hardware barrier for EEG research and
deployment.  However, high benchmark performance does not imply clinical
readiness.  EEG datasets encode acquisition-site, demographic, device, and
labeling biases; a model can exploit these shortcuts.  Clinical use would
require external validation, calibration, uncertainty reporting, subgroup
analysis, and human oversight.  The invertibility of folding also means it does
not provide privacy protection: identifiable or sensitive waveform information
remains present in the transformed input.
