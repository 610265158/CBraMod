\section{Introduction}

Electroencephalography (EEG) datasets vary simultaneously in montage, channel
count, sampling rate, recording length, subject population, and label space.
Recent EEG foundation models address this heterogeneity with large-scale
self-supervised pretraining and specialized tokenization or attention
mechanisms \citep{yang2024biot,jiang2024labram,wang2024cbramod,elouahidi2025reve}.
Their success has made ``more EEG pretraining'' a natural explanation for
improved transfer.  Yet the downstream architecture and the geometry presented
to that architecture are often changed at the same time.  This makes it hard to
separate the benefit of domain pretraining from the benefit of a better
representation interface.

This motivates our central question: \emph{is vision pretraining a free lunch
for EEG decoding?}  We use ``free lunch'' in a deliberately restricted sense:
reusing publicly available visual weights without collecting EEG pretraining
data or running an EEG self-supervised pretraining stage.  It does not mean
zero computation, universal improvement, or deployment without task-specific
fine-tuning.

We study this confound by comparing EEG-pretrained foundation models with a
generic ImageNet encoder that has no EEG pretraining. The visual baseline uses
a deliberately ordinary backbone. A raw
EEG segment is naturally a matrix with electrodes as rows and time samples as
columns.  Directly passing this matrix to a two-dimensional CNN is unattractive:
the input is extremely wide, often only 6--64 rows high, and standard vision
networks reduce both spatial axes by a factor near 32.  The electrode dimension
can therefore collapse after only a few stages, while a large fraction of the
spatial computation is spent traversing a long time axis.  Resizing the matrix
would change values; a spectrogram would discard phase and introduce analysis
choices. The problem is especially severe for spatially sparse EEG: clinical
recordings may have 4--16 channels, while some motor-imagery and imagined-speech
benchmarks have 64. Dense montages provide finer scalp sampling and can remain unfolded; sparse
montages need a geometry adapter both to preserve signal-bearing height and to
expose structured temporal patterns to local 2D operators.

Our alternative is a lossless index transformation.  For fold factor $P$, we
reshape each channel into groups of $P$ consecutive samples and transpose the
within-group phase into the row axis, mapping $C\times T$ to
$(CP)\times(T/P)$.  We call this operation \emph{temporal folding}.  It has no
learned parameters and is exactly invertible.  However, it changes what a
local convolution means: a vertical step within an electrode advances by one
sample, whereas a horizontal step advances by $P$ samples.  A small 2D kernel
therefore combines fine temporal differences with $P$-spaced context, while
the taller representation carries more nonzero rows through the height
hierarchy.

This viewpoint leads to three questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item When can a lossless geometry adapter make generic ImageNet
    pretraining competitive across heterogeneous EEG tasks?
    \item How do fold factor and induced adjacency change the receptive field
    presented to a hierarchical 2D CNN?
    \item On which tasks does an artificial row geometry fail, revealing the
    need for channel identity, physical coordinates, or EEG pretraining?
\end{enumerate}

We study these questions with EfficientNet-B0, ConvNeXt-Tiny, and ViT-Small
across 12 downstream benchmarks. The comparison reports the full metric set
used by the source
benchmarks and keeps conventional supervised models, BIOT, LaBraM, CBraMod,
and REVE as contextual baselines.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A lossless EEG--vision interface.}  We formulate temporal
    folding as a bijective channel--time reindexing requiring no interpolation,
    filtering, or learned adapter.  The operation compensates for the spatial
    sparsity of low-channel EEG while leaving already-dense 64-channel inputs
    unfolded.
    \item \textbf{Evidence that visual representations transfer to EEG.}  Across
    heterogeneous EEG tasks, ImageNet-initialized EfficientNet-B0 and
    DINOv3-initialized ConvNeXt-Tiny and ViT-Small learn from the same folded
    waveform interface, despite the lack of semantic correspondence between
    natural images and EEG.
    \item \textbf{A broad benchmark comparison.}  We evaluate the visual
    transfer approach across 12 datasets and compare it with published
    supervised EEG architectures and EEG
    foundation models, including CBraMod and REVE, using the shared benchmark
    splits.
\end{itemize}

Our claim is that visual pretraining is a transferable cross-domain prior for
EEG when the representation geometry is aligned.  The transfer is conditional:
EEG-specific foundation models retain advantages on some tasks, but their
advantage is not universal under full supervised fine-tuning.
