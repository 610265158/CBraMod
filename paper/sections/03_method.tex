\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview and problem setting}

An EEG segment is naturally represented as a channel--time tensor
$\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C\times T}$, where $B$ is the batch size,
$C$ is the number of channels, and $T$ is the number of time samples. Our goal
is to reuse an off-the-shelf vision encoder without converting the waveform to
a spectrogram or interpolating it into a conventional image. We therefore
introduce a parameter-free folding operator $F_P$ between the EEG input and
the visual backbone:

\begin{center}
\fbox{%
\begin{minipage}{0.91\linewidth}
\centering
\[
[B,C,T]
\xrightarrow{\;F_P\;}
[B,1,CP,T/P]
\xrightarrow{\;\text{zero padding}\;}
\text{vision backbone}
\xrightarrow{\;\text{task head}\;}
\text{logits}.
\]
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

Temporal folding is the only method-specific component in this pipeline. It
changes the two-dimensional layout presented to the visual encoder while
preserving every preprocessed EEG sample exactly. Here, \emph{lossless}
refers to this folding operator; dataset-specific clipping, scaling, and
backbone padding are separate pipeline operations.

\subsection{Lossless phase-interleaved temporal folding}

Choose an integer fold factor $P$ such that $P\mid T$ and define
$W=T/P$.  The folding operator $F_P$ maps the waveform to a single-channel
image $\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times(CP)\times W}$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fold}
  \mathbf{I}_{1,cP+p,w}
  = \mathbf{X}_{c,wP+p},
  \quad
  0\leq c<C,\ 0\leq p<P,\ 0\leq w<W.
\end{equation}

Rows are ordered by electrode and then by within-group temporal phase.  Thus
all $P$ phase rows from the same electrode remain adjacent.  For example, one
channel with $T=16$ and $P=4$ becomes

\begin{center}
\small
$\begin{bmatrix}
x_0&x_4&x_8&x_{12}\\
x_1&x_5&x_9&x_{13}\\
x_2&x_6&x_{10}&x_{14}\\
x_3&x_7&x_{11}&x_{15}
\end{bmatrix}$.
\end{center}

The inverse map is
$\mathbf{X}_{c,wP+p}=\mathbf{I}_{1,cP+p,w}$, so $F_P$ is a bijection: it does
not interpolate, filter, aggregate, or discard samples. This distinguishes
phase-interleaved folding from signal transformations such as resizing and
time--frequency conversion. It also differs from a contiguous-chunk reshape,
which produces the same output shape but assigns consecutive blocks of the
waveform to rows. Figure~\ref{fig:folding-overview} illustrates the proposed
reindexing on a real 16-channel TUEV segment; additional visualizations are
provided in Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:folding-examples}.

\subsection{Geometry and induced locality}

Folding changes the computation of a local 2D operator even though it preserves
the input values. It increases the signal-bearing height from $C$ to $CP$ and
reduces the width from $T$ to $T/P$. This is useful for sparse EEG montages,
whose channel dimension can otherwise collapse early in a hierarchical vision
backbone with repeated spatial downsampling.

For two folded samples from the same electrode, the image and original-time
distances are respectively
\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{img}}=|p-p'|+|w-w'|,\qquad
d_{\mathrm{time}}=|(w-w')P+(p-p')|.
\end{equation}
A vertical step connects consecutive samples, whereas a horizontal step connects
samples $P$ time points apart. Therefore, a local $k\times k$ convolution
captures temporal offsets of the form $\Delta t=P\Delta w+\Delta p$; for
$P=4$, a $3\times3$ kernel combines the offsets
$\{-5,-4,-3,-1,0,1,3,4,5\}$ around its center. Figure~\ref{fig:folding-overview}
visualizes how this kernel captures both adjacent-sample structure and
$P$-spaced temporal context after folding. Folding therefore combines two
effects: it preserves more signal-bearing height through the backbone and
exposes short- and medium-range temporal relations to local 2D operators.

The induced neighborhood is not universally favorable. Consecutive samples across a phase wrap
are separated across folded columns, and transitions between electrode blocks
depend on channel order rather than physical proximity; Appendix~\ref{app:convolution}
analyzes what a local window reads inside one electrode block and across such
a boundary, where the vertical neighbor is a different electrode rather than an
adjacent sample. Larger $P$ increases
height and temporal-offset diversity but can make these boundaries less local.

\subsection{Geometry-based fold-factor selection}
\label{sec:p-selection}

The fold factor controls this trade-off. For the evaluated sequence lengths, we define a reproducible default from
the input shape and the stride of a standard hierarchical vision backbone:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:p-geometry-rule}
 P_{\mathrm{geo}}=
 \begin{cases}
  1, & C\geq62,\\
  \min\{P\in\mathbb{N}: P\mid T,\ CP\geq64\}, & C<62.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The target of 64 folded rows retains at least two signal-bearing cells after a
total spatial stride of 32. Native channel counts close to this target are
treated as sufficient, so the 62-channel SEED-V input remains unfolded. The
rule uses only $(C,T)$ and backbone geometry, and is fixed before training
without consulting validation or test performance. Selecting the smallest
valid factor also preserves as much horizontal resolution as possible.

Equation~\ref{eq:p-geometry-rule} is a reproducible geometric prior rather
than a claim that 64 rows or the selected $P$ is universally optimal. The
preferred temporal scale may depend on the task and backbone; we evaluate this
sensitivity with validation-controlled fold-factor sweeps in
Appendix~\ref{app:p-ablation}.
